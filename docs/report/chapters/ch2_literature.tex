\chapter{LITERATURE SURVEY}

\section{Overview}
The literature relevant to ARGUS is organised along five dimensions of the problem: how the money is represented (graph structure and time), where labelled training data comes from and how large it is, how robust a detector is when the criminal fights back, how to learn when labels are rare or scarce, and what unit of detection is handed to an investigator. Ten papers were studied; each contributes one building block to ARGUS, and each leaves part of the problem open.

\section{Representation: Graphs and Time}
Johannessen and Jullum \cite{johannessen2025finding} build a heterogeneous graph of customers, accounts and banks from real bank data and extend message-passing GNNs to aggregate neighbour messages separately for each edge type; it is the first heterogeneous GNN for AML and the base paper of this project. Its data is private and from a single bank, and it has no temporal or adversarial evaluation. Di Gennaro et al. \cite{digennaro2024amatriciana} (Amatriciana) keep the whole transaction graph intact and combine a GraphSAGE encoder with an LSTM over each account's transaction sequence, trained with a class-weighted Matthews-correlation loss; on IBM synthetic AML data they report F1 0.76, AUC 0.82 and 55\% fewer false positives than plain GraphSAGE. Alarab and Prakoonwit \cite{alarab2023graph} reach the same conclusion independently on Bitcoin data: a temporal GCN combined with an LSTM outperforms static models on the Elliptic graph, so time carries signal that static graphs miss.

\textbf{ARGUS adopts} the heterogeneous account--entity--bank schema, temporal transfer edges, and benchmarks GNN variants (GINE, GATv2, GraphSAGE) instead of assuming one backbone.

\section{Data and Scale}
Labelled laundering data cannot be bought. Granados et al. \cite{granados2024gan} build it with an agent-based model of normal customers, mules and coordinators following behavioural rules (receive, hold, split, forward), wrapped in a GAN for realism; every generated trajectory has a known label. Weber et al. \cite{weber2018scalable} generate a 1-million-node AML graph with the AMLSim simulator and show that plain GCNs hit compute and memory limits that neighbour sampling relieves; their accuracy numbers are preliminary. The IBM AML datasets \cite{altman2023realistic} used in this project are descendants of this line of simulators.

\textbf{ARGUS adopts} injected rings with known ground truth as the basis of its digital twin, and mini-batch neighbour sampling from the start.

\section{Robustness: When the Criminal Fights Back}
Prasetya et al. \cite{prasetya2025multi} play a multi-round game on promotion fraud: the detector predicts, the attacker regenerates fraud on the evolving graph, and the cycle repeats. Detectors that start near 0.9 F1 fall below 0.56 F1 across rounds; their temporally pre-trained embedder is more robust. Dou et al. \cite{dou2020enhancing} (CARE-GNN) identify \emph{feature camouflage} and \emph{relation camouflage} --- fraudsters surrounding themselves with legitimate neighbours --- as attacks on the aggregation step of GNNs, and counter them with label-aware neighbour filtering trained by reinforcement learning.

\textbf{ARGUS adopts} the multi-round attack-and-retrain protocol as its core experiment, and uses the camouflage taxonomy as its attacker's playbook. Neither paper is about bank AML, and neither measures recovery after retraining.

\section{Learning under Constraints: Rare and Scarce Labels}
Liu et al. \cite{liu2021pick} (PC-GNN) show that at very low positive rates a model can score high accuracy while catching nobody, and propose label-balanced and neighbourhood sampling. Cardoso et al. \cite{cardoso2022laundrograph} (LaundroGraph) pre-train a GNN self-supervised on a customer--transaction graph, improving AUC by 12 percentage points over the best non-graph baseline while assisting analysts rather than replacing them.

\textbf{ARGUS adopts} PR-AUC and minority-class F1 as the honest metrics (never accuracy), validation-chosen thresholds, and an analyst-assist design in which the system recommends and an officer decides.

\section{Detection Target: What the Investigator Receives}
Bellei et al. \cite{bellei2024shape} release Elliptic2, with 122{,}000 labelled subgraphs over a 49-million-node Bitcoin graph, and show that laundering is better treated as a subgraph problem than a node problem.

\textbf{ARGUS adopts} ring-level evaluation (ring recall, reconstruction Jaccard) alongside transaction-level metrics, because investigators act on rings, not single accounts.

\section{Research Gap}
Each result above was established once, in isolation and often on different data (Bitcoin, promotion fraud, review fraud) with no shared ground truth. Table~\ref{tab:gap} marks which properties each paper covers. No surveyed work combines a heterogeneous, temporal, imbalance-aware AML detector, evaluated at transaction and ring level inside a multi-bank testbed with complete ground truth, and measures how much an adaptive, camouflaged attacker removes ($D$) and how much retraining wins back ($R$). ARGUS is designed to fill exactly this cluster.
